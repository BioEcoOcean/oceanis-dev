### 1.1 What Is OCEANIS? {#what-is-oceanis}

OCEANIS is a collaborative, **non-prescriptive planning tool** to not only support integrated ocean action, but support holistic thinking for any initiative. It is a circular **framework** of eight interconnected components commonly related to aspects of project planning and implementation (Fig. 1). We describe the framework in detail in [Chapter 3](#chapter-3). Developed through **three-year co-creative process**, the framework has received contributions from hundreds of individuals across disciplines and career stages, coordinated through the European Union-funded project, BioEcoOcean (Grant Agreement No. 101136748).

We chose the name OCEANIS intentionally to provide a unique identification with little to no pre-existing associations. OCEANIS was originally named the "Blueprint for Integrated Ocean Science", often simply referred to as "the Blueprint". For a history outlining the development of OCEANIS, see [Section 1.2](#origin-and-development).

#### OCEANIS Usage

OCEANIS can be used in different ways to support individuals or groups to reflect on their work, think holistically, and create action plans for any aspect of a project, programme, proposal, or other initiative. The framework is not meant to prescribe solutions. OCEANIS workshops are meant to create a shared space to:

* identify the current state of the work
* raise questions
* brainstorm solutions
* explore cross-domain perspectives
* establish a collaborative space where all voices can be heard
* explore connections that might otherwise be overlooked
* create action plans

OCEANIS is especially meant to encourage holistic thinking and support discussions for areas we may be less familiar with or do not often engage with. Often, we may not even know what questions to ask or where to begin. OCEANIS supports us in strengthening familiarity and giving us direction on what to questions to. We cover this topic in more detail in [Chapter 2](#chapter-2).

> **Key point**
>
> OCEANIS is intentionally non-directive. It is not meant to provide answers, but to guide users in asking thoughtful questions, making connections across domains, and arriving at potential solutions and next steps together.
{: .manual-callout}

### 1.2 Origin and Development of OCEANIS {#origin-and-development}

OCEANIS grew out of the recognition that ocean observation increasingly requires integration across disciplines (e.g., biological, physical, biogeochemical), yet we rarely have structured frameworks to do this productively. Its development heavily focused on a co-creation process, involving a series of co-creation workshops, usability testing, and iterative refinement including feedback across various institution types and career stages. We want OCEANIS to support the transformation of the ocean observing community **from a scattered network operating largely in silos** (like a disorganised container of Lego blocks, where each piece is valuable, but disconnected) **into a system of interconnected and interacting processes** (Fig. 3). Structured guidelines make it much easier to build the Lego structure, and they also make it easier to build together. Similarly, by using OCEANIS as a guiding framework, the community can better see and strengthen links between and within the ocean observing system. Together, we can build a more cohesive and integrated ocean observing community, much like assembling an intricate Lego castle as a team. This approach will create an observing system and a community that is greater than the sum of its parts.

#### OCEANIS Development

OCEANIS began as a support tool to enhance collaboration, particularly across the ocean observing community. Its goal was to promote a more integrated approach for collecting, managing, and applying biological and ecosystem observations. From the outset, it was conceived as a flexible, question-based framework designed to stimulate critical thinking and foster shared understanding, rather than prescribe a fixed sequence of actions.

Led by the BioEcoOcean project, the framework was initially grounded in a comprehensive literature review (<https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15658184>) and analysis of existing ocean observing frameworks, conducted in early 2024. This review identified persistent challenges around coordination, integration, and usability, and helped define where a new tool could add real value. It underscored the need to connect technical workflows with societal demands, a principle that remains central to OCEANIS today.

What began as a linear, step-by-step structure quickly evolved (Fig. 2). It became clear early in development that a linear design could not adequately capture the complexity and interconnectedness of the ocean observing system, or any other system. This led to the spiderweb configuration, first presented publicly at the UN Ocean Decade Conference in Barcelona in April 2024. The spiderweb reflects the non-sequential, iterative nature of ocean observing work and the importance of flexibility across all its components.

The framework has been shaped throughout by an extensive co-creation process. Internal consortium workshops, an international stakeholder survey reaching participants from 68 countries, focus groups at major conferences, and dedicated workshops with bodies such as the GOOS BioEco Panel have all contributed to its evolution. This process expanded the framework from an initial seven components to the current nine, adding Evaluation, Communication & Outreach, and Application in Society in response to stakeholder feedback. This reflects a growing recognition that societal relevance and public engagement are as integral to ocean observing as the technical work.

### 1.3 Who Is OCEANIS For? {#who-is-oceanis-for}

OCEANIS was designed with ocean observing systems specifically in mind but has since been tested and applied in broader contexts. As a framework, it can be used more generally and is easily used by a wide range of users, including:

* Ocean scientists, data managers, and researchers working on interdisciplinary projects
* Project managers and coordinators developing proposals or implementation plans
* Individuals developing personal projects
* Policy practitioners and NGOs engaging with ocean data and evidence
* Educators facilitating cross-domain learning in graduate or professional settings
* Teams at any career stage who want to think more holistically about their work

Really, the OCEANIS framework is for anyone who wishes to strengthen their work and practice thinking holistically!

### 1.4 Why Is OCEANIS Beneficial? {#why-is-oceanis-beneficial}

OCEANIS provides a **structured framework and pathway** to explore cross-domain solutions and support **moving beyond siloed thinking**. It creates an intentional space to connect perspectives, broaden understanding, and collaboratively develop action plans.

The framework encourages us to ask questions that are meant to be addressed collaboratively, often with people from different areas of expertise. The idea is not to have an answer for every question, but to identify those that are relevant and require further investigation. Questions inspire us to not only think about familiar aspects, but also new considerations and to encourage the involvement of relevant people (for example, a modeler, a communicator, and a strategist) to work together.

Traditional project planning often proceeds component by component (data collection, then analysis, then communication), often without considering the connections between these stages. OCEANIS provides a structured alternative by:

* Creating intentional space for cross-domain thinking
* Enabling identification of blind spots and gaps that siloed planning misses
* Facilitating collaborative action planning across diverse expertise
* Encouraging reflection on societal relevance, evaluation, and communication from the outset
* Providing a reusable framework that can be revisited at any project stage

We have also leveraged co-design to identify relevant resources to support planning for each OCEANIS component. See the [OCEANIS Resources repository](resources.html).

> **Key point**
>
> OCEANIS helps us identify and ask questions that we might never have thought to ask on our own - because **we can't ask about things we are not aware of**.

### 1.5 Examples of OCEANIS Applications {#example-use-cases}

The OCEANIS framework is intentionally flexible and can be used in many different ways, from large collaborative workshops to informal individual reflection. It works equally well to facilitate group exercises, smaller conversations within teams, or simply as a reference document on the side of your desk.

Below, we provide example use cases that illustrate the varied contexts the OCEANIS framework can be used for. Each example includes suggested geographic and temporal scope, where applicable.

#### Project planning

At the outset of a new project, use OCEANIS to ensure planning covers all dimensions of the work, not just data collection and analysis, but also how the work is relevant to various societal actors, how results will be communicated during and at the end of the project, how data management will be handled, and what kind of data may support future analyses and data product development. This helps us identify potential gaps early, before they become costly, and helps us avoid the stress of, for example, retrofitting data to models that require higher resolution than the original sample collection.

#### Conference preparation

Before attending a major conference or stakeholder meeting, use OCEANIS as an individual or a small team to generate a focused set of questions you want to explore with a broader audience. Working through the framework in advance helps us identify gaps and uncertainties in our own thinking, areas we may want to explore with others, and gives us a structured way to turn conversations into productive collaborations.

#### Addressing a societal need

When responding to a specific societal challenge, such as supporting a coastal community’s management decisions, OCEANIS can help a team connect the technical work to the people it is meant to serve. It prompts questions about who needs the information, in what form, by when, how often, what may already be working, and what resources are available to collect the information.

#### Identifying workflow gaps

Use OCEANIS to evaluate an existing programme or workflow by working through each component and asking where things are working well and where they are not. This is particularly useful for identifying disconnects between components, for example, data being collected but not managed in a way that makes it FAIR and/or usable for analysis or decision-making.

#### Evaluating a current system or project

At a project milestone or end point, OCEANIS provides a structured framework for reflection. Rather than evaluating outputs alone, it encourages teams to assess whether all components were adequately addressed, what connections were missed, and what could be done differently next time.

#### Gaining a holistic overview

For teams new to a system, or for individuals stepping into a new role, OCEANIS offers a way to map the full landscape of an initiative. It provides us with a shared framework that eases the burden of getting up to speed, whether entering new system or reviewing a previous monitoring period. Working through the spiderweb together also builds shared understanding of how different parts of the system relate to one another, and may highlight the fact that team members may approach the same work from different, valuable perspectives.

#### Strengthening stakeholder communication

Use OCEANIS to facilitate dialogue across sectors, disciplines, or institutions that do not often talk to one another. The shared visual language of the spiderweb gives participants a common reference point, making it easier to identify where responsibilities overlap, where communication is breaking down, and where new connections could be built. It can also help us understand how different team members naturally prioritise work. For example, team members from one sector may approach the spiderweb from a Data Collection-first perspective, while another may lead with Societal Relevance. This makes those difference visible and easier to discuss.

#### Designing a research study or experiment

When designing controlled laboratory experiments, OCEANIS is useful for stress-testing designs. It prompts questions that are easy to overlook when the focus is on the science itself. Are our methods appropriate for our question, reproducible and well-documented? Do we have a data management plan fit for purpose? Who are our results intended for, and how will we communicate our findings beyond our immediate team? Working through these questions early can save significant time and effort later.

#### Improving data pipelines and analytical workflows

For data managers, analysts, and modellers, OCEANIS can help surface the upstream and downstream dependencies that affect the quality and usability of data products. It helps technical teams ask not just how a pipeline works, but why it is structured that way, and whether the outputs are actually reaching and serving their intended users. It can also prompt reflection on how results are being communicated, and whether gaps in communication may be preventing product or data uptake.

#### Individual reflection on a thesis or personal project

OCEANIS does not require a group. As an individual - whether a student shaping a thesis chapter, a researcher scoping a new line of inquiry, or someone early in a project trying to get their bearings - working through the Spiderweb alone can be a surprisingly clarifying exercise. It helps us see where our energy and planning are concentrated, and where we may be assuming things will “just work out.” A student might find, for example, that they have thought carefully about data collection and analysis but have barely considered data management, communication, or how their work connects to broader societal questions. OCEANIS won’t write the thesis for us, but it will help us ask better questions of it.
